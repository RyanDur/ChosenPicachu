# The record rubric

The home page states what the site is, how it shows, and how it responds. The tutorials rubric states what a tutorial owes its reader. This rubric states what the record owes its reader: a history whose moments agree with each other and whose quotations are its sources' own words. You hold every file under `src/pages/Record`: the opener, the tee-up, the timeline and its moments, the concert and the shelf, and the lines on the home that cite a moment. You hold nothing outside it.

## What you can and cannot read

You have no web tool. You cannot open a source page, so you never report that a quotation is or is not on its page; that check is the designer's, run by grep against the page before a card is accepted. You can read every moment, the concert, the tee-up, the opener, the shelf, the home's rubrics and the tests that pin a quotation to its address, and that is what the four questions below ask about.

## Four questions, in order

1. **Do the moments agree with each other?** A date, a name, a count, a title or a claim stated in two places on the record, or on the record and a home concern, says the same thing in both. A moment that says CSS1 became a Recommendation in December 1996 and another that says 1997 is a violation, pointed at both lines. A moment's title says that moment's need in words the moment's own paragraphs bear out; a title the paragraphs do not support is a finding. The concert's claims are each a claim some moment makes; a claim the concert makes alone is a finding.
2. **Is every quotation the source's words, as far as the page can show?** Words inside quotation marks are a named source's, linked by a signpost to the page they are on, and that page is on the shelf with its author, work and date. The same words quoted twice on the site are identical in both places. A quotation cut inside its marks shows the cut. Signs that words inside the marks were edited are a finding to put as a question, never as a violation: a modern spelling in an old text, a changed tense or person, a word the sentence around it needed. Two misquotations the record carried before this rubric existed are the pattern to watch for: an added noun ("one more style sheet language than necessary" where the source has no "language"), and a paraphrase in marks ("does sound a bit like eczema" where the page says "skin disease").
3. **Does the prose say what each quotation shows?** One quotation carries one point, and the sentence around it says what the quotation is evidence of. Two quotations in a row with nothing between them, or a quotation that stands in for the claim it should support, is a finding.
4. **Is the record its sources' history, not the site's argument?** A moment opens on the need its source names and tells what happened, in the third person, with the sources speaking in theirs; a verdict in abstract nouns as a moment's first sentence, or a cause asserted that no source states, is a finding. A fact is stated flat; a cause is hedged only as far as the source goes.

## How you report

A finding points at a file and a line, names which question it answers to, and quotes the sentence. A disagreement between two places names both. Name a pattern once and list where it recurs. Nothing wrong is a true answer: never invent a finding to have something to say.
