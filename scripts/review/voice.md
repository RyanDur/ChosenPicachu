# The voice rubric

The home page states what the site is for and what each language does; the record proves it from the sources; the tutorials teach. This rubric states how all of that is written: in one voice, the author's, in the manner of Martin Fowler and Kent Beck. Fowler names a thing, says what he means by the name, gives one example from real work, and says what the thing costs and when he would not use it. Beck states a rule in under ten words, then the case for it, then what he does. Both write "I", credit people by name, admit what they do not know, and end on the practical thing. Neither performs, and neither scolds. Ryan named them as the voice on 2026-10-17 and said the voice is not a matter of counts: read the text aloud as its author and ask whether a careful engineer would say it to a colleague.

## What you can and cannot read

You read the words as they stand in the components and the data they draw from (`src/pages/**/*.tsx`, `src/pages/names.json`, the moments and stories under `src/pages/Record`). You have no web tool, so you never judge whether a quotation is on its page or whether a claim about the field is true; those are the record rubric's and the designer's. You judge how the prose reads.

## Seven questions, in order

1. **Does each paragraph make one point, and open on something doing something?** Its first sentence names a person, a document or a thing acting, or picks up what the paragraph before ended on; it is never a verdict in abstract nouns ("the method was the point", "what the separation bought was control"). A sentence that needs a breath in the middle is two sentences; a chain of fragments on semicolons is a list pretending to be a sentence.
2. **Does it state the fact before it builds on it?** The reader learned the three languages one at a time and on the job. A sentence that only makes sense to someone who already knows the thing ("its behavior comes with it") says the thing first. A term is defined where it first appears and used plainly after.
3. **Does it say the thing, then the reason, and claim only what is true?** Every claim that could be questioned carries its because, a fact about the code, the platform or the source, never a feeling about them. A fact is stated flat, a cause is hedged only as far as the evidence goes, and a cleaner line is never bought with a wider claim ("much" where the evidence supports much, not "most"; no "every page here was built that way" that the reader cannot check). A verb claims only what is true: "putting the job where it belongs" does not say the job was there before, "giving the job back" does.
4. **Is it the author's voice, without blame or performance?** "I" where the author chooses, "we" only where it means the trade or the reader working alongside; the record in the third person with its sources in theirs. What a thing does, not what someone failed to do ("a div standing in for a button", not "a div where a button was meant"); no grievance against code, browsers or readers. No puns, coinages or figures where a plain phrase exists, no mannered phrasing where the trade has a plain word, no closing line that gestures at depth or legacy, no em dashes.
5. **Are trades told as trades and preferences as preferences?** Where working developers disagree, each side's gains and costs are stated and the author's own choice is in the first person, ranked above nothing. A rule the site states holds for every side it names, and appears once on the home and once in the story that earns it.
6. **Is the depth where it belongs?** The home gives each language's job, one example, its cost and where to read more; mechanism that needs its own example belongs on Practice or in a demo. A page or a tab opens on what the thing is, the problem a developer meets with it, and the page's view, before anything is built; only the site's opening carries a why.
7. **Does it flow, read aloud?** Each paragraph has a bridge in and a bridge out. A list of claims with no turn reads as a brochure; an example with no lead-in reads as a non sequitur. Two options in one sentence shape let the reader hear the contrast. A pronoun points at something in reach. The same phrase three times in a paragraph drones; the same word meaning two things in one sentence names the second. A trailing phrase, two prepositions in a row or a clause bolted on after the verb catches the ear: it becomes its own short sentence or goes.

## How you report

A finding points at a file and a line, names which question it answers to, quotes the sentence, and says what catches, as a reader hears it; it offers a rewrite only when one plain sentence shows the fix. A pattern is named once with the places it recurs. Wording is Ryan's to decide: a finding is a concern or a note, never a violation, unless a sentence states as fact what the page cannot show (question 3) or the record speaks in the first person (question 4). Nothing about counts: sentence lengths and word tallies are a tool for a long file, not a finding.
